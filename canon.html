How to read this page. Every division and book of the Pali Canon appears below — nothing is skipped, including the books nobody recommends to beginners. Each entry says what the text actually is in plain contemporary English, tags it to the four curriculum stages (Stage 1 predicament · Stage 2 mechanics · Stage 3 path · Stage 4 relational), and flags where English translation or modern interpretation doesn't map cleanly onto the Pali. One honest global caveat: these are my study summaries, not scholarship — every entry links to full translations so you can check me.

Basket I — Vinaya Piṭaka

The discipline: law code, case history, and constitution of the monastic community.

1. Suttavibhaṅga — the rules analyzed

Mahāvibhaṅga (227 monks' rules) + Bhikkhunīvibhaṅga (311 nuns' rules)

The Pātimokkha rules with everything a court would need: the origin story behind each rule, word-by-word definition of its terms, amendment history, case precedents, and non-offense clauses. It reads like annotated case law — and doubles as the most candid social history we have of the early community, since every rule memorializes someone's failure. All 227 monks' rules with their origin stories are on this site. The nuns' analysis adds the rules specific to bhikkhunīs.

Stage 3Stage 4

Sīla made institutional — and a live record of the knowing-doing gap Stage 3 targets.

Lost in translation The nuns' code includes eight garudhammas subordinating the nuns' order to the monks'. Whether these go back to the Buddha or were inserted by later (male) editors is one of the sharpest live disputes in Buddhist studies — modern bhikkhunī ordination movements turn on it. Translations that present them flatly as "what the Buddha laid down" are taking a side.

2. Khandhakas: Mahāvagga — the great chapter

10 chapters of procedure and founding narrative

The community's operating manual, wrapped around its origin story. It opens with the weeks after the awakening, the first sermon (SN 56.11's home turf), and the first conversions, then legislates everything a functioning organization needs: ordination procedure, the fortnightly recitation meeting (uposatha), the annual rains retreat, robe-making and the kathina ceremony, medicines, food, and schism management. Modern eyes will recognize HR policy, an events calendar, and a conflict-resolution playbook — the Kosambī quarrel chapter is a case study in a community almost destroying itself over a triviality (a monk's bathroom etiquette).

Stage 3Stage 4

Stage 3's environment-engineering principle at full scale; the quarrel narratives are Stage 4 conflict material.

3. Khandhakas: Cūḷavagga — the lesser chapter

12 chapters: procedures, penalties, and two pivotal histories

The sequel volume: disciplinary procedures and probation, dispute-settlement in detail, lodgings and furniture, duties between teachers and students — and two chapters of enormous historical weight. Chapter 10 recounts the founding of the nuns' order: Mahāpajāpatī's request, the Buddha's initial refusal, Ānanda's advocacy ("are women capable of awakening?" — yes), and the conditions imposed. Chapters 11–12 describe the First Council (500 monks reciting the canon from memory just after the Buddha's death) and the Second (the Vesālī dispute over ten relaxed practices, about a century later) — the canon narrating its own creation.

Stage 3Stage 4

Community mechanics and repair — Stage 4 in procedural form.

Lost in translation In the same passage that admits women, the Buddha allegedly predicts the teaching will now last 500 instead of 1,000 years. The prediction failed on its own terms (we're past 2,500), and many scholars read the whole passage as a later interpolation reflecting institutional anxieties, not the Buddha's view. English translations rarely flag this.

4. Parivāra — the appendix

19 chapters of summaries, catechisms, and cross-indexes

A study guide to the previous books, compiled in Sri Lanka possibly centuries later: the rules re-sorted by category, offense, and location, quiz-style question chains, and mnemonic verses. Nobody reads it devotionally; it exists so Vinaya masters could be examined. Its honest modern analogue is the bar-exam outline. Its value today is mostly evidential — it shows the tradition building tooling around its own texts.

Basket II — Sutta Piṭaka

The discourses: five collections (nikāyas), from long set-pieces to one-line verses.

5. Dīgha Nikāya — the long discourses

34 suttas in 3 books

The showpieces: long, literary, often staged as debates with brahmins and rival teachers, and clearly built in part to win an ancient audience. Landmarks: DN 1 (Brahmajāla) catalogs 62 wrong views like a field guide to speculative overreach; DN 2 walks a king through the entire path in one evening; DN 16 (Mahāparinibbāna) narrates the Buddha's last journey and death — old, sick, still teaching, final words "all conditioned things decay; strive diligently"; DN 22 is the long satipaṭṭhāna discourse; DN 31 (Sigālovāda) is the lay-ethics classic — friendship, money, marriage, employment — a household code with no monasticism in it.

Stage 1Stage 3Stage 4

DN 16 is Stage 1's impermanence lesson at maximum scale; DN 31 anchors Stage 3–4 lay practice.

Study notes on this site: DN 1 the net of views · DN 2 fruits of practice · DN 15 great causes · DN 16 the Buddha's last days · DN 22 satipaṭṭhāna · DN 26 the wheel-turning king · DN 31 advice to Sigāla

Lost in translation The Dīgha contains heavy cosmology — deva realms, world cycles, the wheel-turning monarch. A modern reader must make an interpretive choice the text won't make for them: literal cosmology, inherited Indian furniture, or psychological allegory. All three readings have serious defenders; flat English prose makes the mythic register sound like it claims the same kind of truth as the psychology, which the Pali's shift in genre and tone arguably signals against.

6. Majjhima Nikāya — the middle-length discourses

152 suttas in 3 books

The working core of the teaching and the usual reading entry point: concrete scenes, named interlocutors, arguments you can follow. Here live the raft and water-snake similes (MN 22 — even true teachings wrongly grasped injure you), the honey-ball analysis of mental proliferation (MN 18), the Buddha's autobiography of his own search and austerities (MN 26, 36), his parenting — teaching seven-year-old Rāhula truthfulness with a water dipper (MN 61), the saw simile's outrageous standard for goodwill (MN 21), breath meditation (MN 118), the shorter emptiness discourse (MN 121), and the path analyzed factor by factor (MN 141). If the canon were triaged to one collection, most teachers would keep this one.

The only book that carries all four stages at full strength.

7. Saṁyutta Nikāya — the linked discourses

~7,700 mostly short suttas in 56 thematic chapters, 5 books

The canon's filing cabinet: thousands of short texts sorted by topic, which makes it the doctrine's engine room. SN 12 is dependent origination — the full causal mechanics of how suffering assembles itself; SN 22 is the aggregates and non-self (including SN 22.59, the second sermon); SN 35 the six senses; SN 36 feeling-tone (the two arrows); SN 45–56 the path itself, factor by factor, ending with SN 56.11, the first sermon. The first book (Sagāthāvagga) is verse dialogues with gods and Māra — the tempter here reads less like a devil and more like the voice of doubt and self-sabotage, showing up at bad moments and being recognized: "I know you, Māra." Repetitive by design — these were oral flashcards.

Stage 1Stage 2Stage 3

Stage 2's primary source: craving, non-self, and dependent origination live here.

Study notes on this site: SN 3.8 dearer than self · SN 5.2 Somā's answer · SN 12.2 dependent origination · SN 12.61 the monkey mind · SN 15.3 the tears · SN 22.59 not-self · SN 35.28 the fire sermon · SN 36.6 the two arrows · SN 45.2 friendship · SN 46.54 the brahmavihāras · SN 47.19 the acrobats · SN 56.11 the first sermon

Lost in translation Key SN vocabulary resists English: saṅkhāra gets rendered "formations," "fabrications," "volitional activities," or "choices" depending on translator — each choice changes what dependent origination seems to say. And nibbāna literally means a fire going out; in Indian fire-physics a flame that goes out was thought released from its fuel, not annihilated. "Extinction" imports a nihilistic flavor the metaphor didn't carry.

8. Aṅguttara Nikāya — the numbered discourses

~8,000 suttas in 11 chapters, sorted by number of items (ones through elevens)

Teachings filed by list length — ones, twos, threes, up to elevens — which sounds bureaucratic and is actually the most practical, lay-facing collection in the canon. Here are the Kālāma discourse (AN 3.65 — don't accept teachings on tradition, scripture, or charisma; test them against experience and the wise), the five daily recollections (AN 5.57), the lute-string balance (AN 6.55), advice on money, marriage, business ethics, and friendship (AN 8.54 is essentially a personal-finance talk), the eight worldly winds (AN 8.6), and mettā's eleven benefits (AN 11.15). If MN is for understanding, AN is for running a life.

Stage 1Stage 3Stage 4

Stage 3's daily-implementation handbook and much of Stage 4's relational material.

Study notes on this site: AN 3.65 the Kālāmas · AN 3.99 the lump of salt · AN 4.62 four happinesses · AN 5.57 five recollections · AN 5.161 removing resentment · AN 5.177 five trades · AN 5.198 well-spoken speech · AN 6.55 the lute strings · AN 7.64 anger's seven gifts to enemies · AN 8.6 the worldly winds · AN 8.54 lay prosperity · AN 11.15 mettā's benefits

Lost in translation The Kālāma Sutta is the most over-claimed text in modern Buddhism — quoted as "believe nothing, question everything," a charter for free-thinking skepticism. The actual discourse ends with the Kālāmas taking refuge, and its test isn't private opinion but "what the wise praise" plus observed consequences. It licenses empiricism, not relativism — the popular paraphrase is a 20th-century remix.

The Khuddaka Nikāya — the "minor" collection, book by book

Fifteen books in the Sinhalese reckoning — from the canon's oldest poetry to its latest hagiography. "Minor" refers to format, not importance.

9. Khuddakapāṭha — the short passages

9 short texts

A pocket handbook for new practitioners, probably compiled as a chanting primer: the refuges, the ten precepts, a body contemplation, and three of the most-recited texts in the Buddhist world — the Maṅgala Sutta (a layperson's list of true blessings: good friends, honest work, caring for family), the Ratana Sutta (protective verses), and the Metta Sutta. Functionally the canon's onboarding document.

10. Dhammapada — verses on the teaching

423 verses in 26 chapters

The most translated Buddhist text on earth: aphorisms on mind, craving, anger, and freedom, sharp enough to survive any century. "Mind precedes all experience"; "hatred never ends by hatred"; "you are your own protector." It compresses Stages 1 and 2 into memorizable form, which is exactly what it was for — portable wisdom for people who couldn't carry books.

Stage 1Stage 2
Lost in translation Verse 1's famous "mind precedes all things / we become what we think" flattens mano­pubbaṅgamā dhammā — where dhammā more likely means "mental experiences" than "all things." The popular reading turns a precise psychological claim into law-of-attraction metaphysics. With 100+ English versions in print, the Dhammapada is where translation drift is easiest to see: compare any two renderings of chapter 1 and you'll find different philosophies.

11. Udāna — inspired utterances

80 short narratives, each capped by a verse

Eighty scenes that moved the Buddha to spontaneous verse. Includes the blind men and the elephant (rival theorists each grasping one part and brawling over the whole — the canon's best epistemology joke) and the instruction to Bāhiya, one of the tersest teachings anywhere: "in the seen, only the seen; in the heard, only the heard" — perception without the added self, Stage 2 in eighteen words.

12. Itivuttaka — "thus it was said"

112 short suttas, prose + verse

Attributed to the memory of Khujjuttarā — a laywoman, a servant, and one of the canon's certified master memorizers, who heard the Buddha in the palace and re-taught the harem. Short, direct teachings on craving, goodwill, and generosity ("if beings knew the fruit of giving as I do, they would not eat without sharing"). Notable both for content and provenance: a book of the canon transmitted through a working-class woman's recall.

13. Suttanipāta — the sutta collection

71 poems in 5 chapters

Some of the oldest material in the entire canon. Its final two chapters (Aṭṭhakavagga, Pārāyanavagga) are quoted by name elsewhere in the canon itself and show a strikingly early voice: minimal doctrine, hostile to clinging to views — even Buddhist views — and to religious quarreling. Also home to the Metta Sutta, the Rhinoceros Horn poem on solitude, and the Buddha's flat rejection of caste: worth is made by conduct, not birth (Snp 1.7, 3.9).

Stage 2Stage 4
Lost in translation The Aṭṭhakavagga's polemic against "views" (diṭṭhi) reads in English like anti-intellectualism. The target is narrower: identity-fused positions — opinions worn as self, defended like territory. The philosophical puzzle of how this squares with "Right View" as path factor #1 is real and old; translators who render both words identically hide the tension instead of exposing it.

14. Vimānavatthu — mansion stories

85 verse stories

Interviews with radiant gods in celestial mansions, each asked: what did you do to earn this? The answers are almost embarrassingly small — offered water, gave a seat, spoke honestly — which is the actual teaching: ordinary generosity compounds. This is devotional merit literature, later in style, and few modern teachers assign it; read it as the tradition's motivational poster art — the karmic imagination in full color.

15. Petavatthu — ghost stories

51 verse stories

The companion volume in a minor key: hungry ghosts (petas) explaining the stinginess, fraud, or cruelty that produced their state, and the relief that comes when living relatives make offerings on their behalf. Source of the merit-transfer rituals still central to Theravada funerals. As psychology it lands squarely: the miser's afterlife is simply the miser's inner life, made visible.

Stage 1Stage 3
Lost in translation Merit transfer sits awkwardly with the suttas' own insistence that beings inherit their own kamma (AN 5.57). The commentaries engineered reconciliations (the ghost rejoices, and the rejoicing is its own act), but the seam is visible — this layer of the canon answers popular religious need, not systematic doctrine, and translations smooth over the difference in genre.

16. Theragāthā — verses of the elder monks

264 poems, 1,279 verses

First-person poetry by early monks: ex-princes, ex-laborers, a former executioner, a reformed serial killer (Aṅgulimāla). Depression, lust, backsliding, the urge to quit, and breakthrough — documented in individual voices, not doctrine. Tālapuṭa's long poem arguing with his own mind ("for years you begged me to ordain; now that I have, why do you drag me back?") may be the most relatable text in the canon for anyone who has tried to keep any resolution.

17. Therīgāthā — verses of the elder nuns

73 poems, 522 verses

The world's oldest anthology of women's spiritual literature, and it holds nothing back: Kisāgotamī and the mustard seed (grief for a dead child met not with doctrine but with the discovery that no house is free of death); Paṭācārā, who lost her entire family in one day and went mad before finding the path; ex-courtesans on the decay of the beauty that had been their livelihood; and Somā's retort to Māra's taunt that women lack the wits for awakening: "What does womanhood matter, when the mind is composed and insight present?"

18. Jātaka — birth stories

547 tales of the Buddha's former lives

The Buddha's past lives as monkey-king, deer, merchant, tree spirit — moral fables in which the future Buddha practices generosity, patience, and truthfulness across lifetimes. For most of Buddhist history, most Buddhists met the tradition through these stories, not the suttas: they are the canon's children's literature, folk cinema, and ethics curriculum in one. Strictly, only the verses are canonical; the familiar prose stories are commentary.

Stage 4
Lost in translation Many Jātaka plots are shared pan-Indian folklore (some appear in the Pañcatantra and in Aesop's orbit), retrofitted with a bodhisatta identification. Reading them as biographical claims misses the genre; they are the tradition thinking about virtue in story form — and they're also where values sit that the suttas complicate, like the extreme-giving tales (Vessantara gives away his children) that Buddhist cultures themselves have argued over for centuries.

19. Niddesa — the exposition

Mahāniddesa + Cūḷaniddesa: canonical commentary on parts of the Suttanipāta

A commentary that made it into the canon itself: word-by-word exegesis of the Aṭṭhakavagga and Pārāyanavagga, attributed to Sāriputta. Dry, but historically fascinating — it shows the earliest layer of the tradition already needing to explain its own oldest poems, and it reads those anti-dogmatic verses through a more systematized lens. Watching the Niddesa work is watching interpretation begin.

20. Paṭisambhidāmagga — the path of discrimination

30 treatises of systematic analysis

Proto-Abhidhamma inside the Sutta basket: seventy-three kinds of knowledge, breath meditation in sixteen-step detail, analytical treatments of views, mettā, and liberation. Attributed to Sāriputta, and clearly the bridge between the suttas' teaching style and the Abhidhamma's spreadsheet style. Later Theravada meditation manuals (especially Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga) lean on it heavily — much of "classical Theravada meditation theory" traces here.

21. Apadāna — the legends

~600 autobiographical verse legends

Monks and nuns recount the past-life deeds — usually a small offering to a previous Buddha eons ago — that ripened into their present awakening. Formulaic hagiography from the canon's latest stratum, with a karmic cosmology far more mechanical than the suttas'. Its most interesting angle is sociological: the nuns' apadānas give several major women disciples their fullest backstories anywhere in the canon.

22. Buddhavaṃsa — the chronicle of Buddhas

The lineage of 24 previous Buddhas

Gotama placed in a deep-time lineage: twenty-four prior Buddhas, before each of whom the being who would become "our" Buddha makes or renews his vow — starting as the ascetic Sumedha throwing himself in the mud before Dīpaṅkara. This late book is the seed of the bodhisatta ideal that Mahāyāna would put at the center of everything: awakening as a multi-life project undertaken for all beings. Doctrinally light; historically, a hinge.

23. Cariyāpiṭaka — the basket of conduct

35 verse tales illustrating the perfections

A slim companion to the Buddhavaṃsa: Jātaka episodes retold specifically to illustrate the pāramīs — the perfections of generosity, ethics, renunciation, wisdom, energy, patience, truthfulness, resolve, goodwill, and equanimity. The ten-perfections framework barely appears in the older suttas but became central to later Theravada self-cultivation; this is where it crystallizes. Effectively Stage 4's character-development syllabus in embryo.

Basket III — Abhidhamma Piṭaka

The systematic psychology: experience decomposed into momentary events (dhammas) and their relations. Seven books.

Interpretation flag — read first Theravada tradition holds the Buddha taught the Abhidhamma to his deceased mother in the Tāvatiṁsa heaven, relaying it nightly to Sāriputta. Historical scholarship — and the texts' own style, vocabulary, and absence from the earliest council lists — points to gradual composition in the centuries after the Buddha, systematizing the suttas' terms. Both framings agree on what it is functionally: the tradition's attempt to make the psychology of Stage 2 fully explicit and internally consistent. The map is impressive; the suttas would remind you it is a map.

24. Dhammasaṅgaṇī — enumeration of phenomena

The master taxonomy

The periodic table: every possible type of consciousness classified as wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral, with the mental factors (contact, feeling, attention, energy, mindfulness, greed, aversion…) present in each. The modern analogue is a component model of the mind — each moment of experience is a bundle of factors, and character change means changing which bundles arise. Its opening triad — "wholesome, unwholesome, indeterminate" — frames the entire basket: the analysis is ethical at the root, not neutral description.

Stage 2
Lost in translation Kusala/akusala — "wholesome/unwholesome" (or worse, "good/evil") — literally means skillful/unskillful: competence language, not sin language. The Pali frames mental states as craft to be learned; the standard translations frame them as morality to be judged. That single choice colors the whole Western reception of Buddhist ethics.

25. Vibhaṅga — the book of analysis

18 topics, each analyzed three ways

Eighteen core topics — aggregates, sense bases, dependent origination, satipaṭṭhāna, the path, jhāna — each run through a triple mill: the sutta's way of putting it, the Abhidhamma's stricter redefinition, and a question-and-answer cross-examination. Reading the two registers side by side is the clearest view anywhere of how the scholastic project transformed the discourse language — same words, tightened meanings.

26. Dhātukathā — discussion of elements

14 chapters of combinatorial classification

A short, purely technical book asking one question exhaustively: which phenomena are included in, excluded from, or associated with which aggregates, bases, and elements? Essentially a consistency-checker run over the taxonomy of the first two books — database integrity testing for the model of mind. No one's bedside reading; included because the system needed it.

27. Puggalapaññatti — designation of persons

Personality types, ones through tens

The basket's most readable book, and a deliberate anomaly: after volumes dissolving persons into impersonal events, this one classifies people — the angry type, the envious type, the talker-not-doer, the one quick to see, the one who needs it spelled out, those who fear blame, those restrained only by consequences. A working teacher's typology: diagnosis by temperament so the medicine can be matched to the patient. Its very existence concedes the Stage 2 point that "person" remains an indispensable practical fiction.

28. Kathāvatthu — points of controversy

~200 debated theses, attributed to Moggaliputtatissa (3rd century BCE)

The canon's debate transcript: two-hundred-odd disputed positions held by rival Buddhist schools — Does a "person" ultimately exist? Can an arahant regress? Is everything momentary? — each refuted in a formal logical procedure that is among the oldest systematized argumentation anywhere. Openly dated by tradition itself to the Third Council under Aśoka, two centuries-plus after the Buddha, making it the one book the tradition admits is late. Invaluable as a snapshot of early Buddhism arguing with itself.

29. Yamaka — the book of pairs

10 chapters of paired questions

Logic proofs by exhaustive converse-testing: "Is all form the form-aggregate? Is the form-aggregate all form?" — thousands of such pairs, mapping exactly where terms overlap and where they don't. The purpose is precision: after the Yamaka, no term in the system can be ambiguous. The modern analogue is a formal test suite for definitions. Purely for specialists, and it knows it.

30. Paṭṭhāna — the book of conditional relations

24 modes of conditionality; by far the largest text in the canon

The system's crown: everything the earlier books classified is now put in motion through twenty-four kinds of conditioning relation — root-cause, object, dominance, proximity, mutuality, habit ("repetition condition": every act makes the next like-act easier — the canon's law of habit formation), kamma, nutriment, and more. Fully expanded it is functionally infinite; Burmese tradition reveres it as the deepest of all texts and chants it for protection. Its one-sentence cash value is enormous, though: nothing arises from a single cause; everything arises from webs of conditions — dependent origination generalized into a total worldview.

Where this leaves you. The four stages and the three baskets interlock: Stage 1's predicament is the suttas' opening move everywhere; Stage 2's mechanics live in SN, the Udāna, and the whole Abhidhamma; Stage 3's daily path is AN, the Vinaya, and the meditation manuals; Stage 4's relational training runs from the Metta Sutta through the Jātakas to the perfections. Nothing in the canon falls outside the loop — which is the strongest argument that the four-stage frame carves the tradition at its joints.
